🔔 What is happening in the sky
Changes found in telescope images, turned into events - with when and where to look from your place. Data:

From dry telescope images to things you can go and see

Most tools just show changes in telescope pictures. SKYDAR turns them into events - “something moved”, “a star got brighter”, “new data arrived”, “an asteroid passes through” - puts them as glowing dots on a sky map and tells you when and where to look from your own location. A Telegram bot can alert you.

How to use it

NASA’s SPHEREx maps the whole sky every six months in 102 near-infrared bands. SKYDAR shows the same patch of sky at different epochs like a short video, so moving objects (brown dwarfs, asteroids, nearby stars) and variable stars “jump out”.

1
Look up
On the Sky tab you stand outside and look at tonight’s stars (drag to look around). Tap any star or any spot - or pick a 🎯 mission.
2
Watch what changes
Sequence, blink, swipe, subtract (A − B), add, or the automatic difference layer.
3
Become a scientist
Co-add bands, overlay Gaia / AllWISE, click anywhere for an on-the-fly spectrum, check frame flags.

Embed or share a view

Everything is controlled by the URL, so any view can be linked or embedded:

Parameters: demo (1 = synthetic data, 0 = real SPHEREx data), ra, dec (degrees), fov (0.01–0.5°), det (D1–D6), coadd=1, embed=1 (hides all chrome).

Which stars are on the map?

The sky map shows the stars a person can see with the naked eye (about 5,000 over the whole sky - roughly 2,000 at a time in the darkest places). The galaxy itself holds 100-400 billion stars; telescopes like SPHEREx see far fainter ones than your eyes.

Brightness is measured in magnitude: lower = brighter (Sirius is −1.5, the faintest naked-eye stars about 6). The 🌃 menu on the Sky tab sets how dark your sky is, so you only see what you could really see: city centre (~3.5), suburb (~4.5), countryside (~5.5), very dark sky (~6.5).

Honest about accuracy

Results are candidates, not discoveries: there is no artefact masking or SIMBAD/SkyBoT cross-match, and a SPHEREx pixel is ~6″. “DEMO” marks synthetic data.

The sky map is drawn locally from the ~115 brightest stars (approximate positions, no planets or Moon yet). Data: NASA/IPAC Infrared Science Archive (IRSA) · Gaia (ESA) · AllWISE · DSS2/2MASS via CDS hips2fits.
